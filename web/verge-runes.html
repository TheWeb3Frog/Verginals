INTERFACE PREVIEW

Verge Runes

Fungible tokens on Verge, from the protocol in spec/RUNES-SPEC-v0.md. This page is how the interface could work. Every figure on it is a worked example, not live data.

Checking what the server actually reports…

01

Prove your own balance

Every token metaprotocol has the same hole: you ask an indexer what you own and you take its word for it. The spec closes it. Anyone can publish a merkle root over the whole balance set, on chain, and your wallet checks its own balance against that root. It never has to trust the server that answered it.

So the balance card carries a state, not just a number. This one is real arithmetic: the hashes below are computed in your browser with WebCrypto, over sample leaves.

The design call: unverified is not an error and must not look like one. It means the wallet has not checked yet, or the root for this height is not published. Only a balance that fails against a published root is red, and that is a serious thing to show, so it says who published the root it disagrees with.

02

Claim a ticker

Runes made names free and bots took every good one, paying miners rather than the ecosystem. Here a ticker has a price, set by length, and the point is not revenue: it is that one good name stays affordable to a real project while fifty of them ruin a squatter.

The price is not spent. It is locked for four years and then it is yours again. Every fee model eventually has to say who receives the money, and every answer creates somebody with a stake in the protocol. This one answers nobody: the coins sit in an output only the etcher can open, and only once the wait is over. Waiting is the cost, so fifty names are still ruinous, and the protocol still has no beneficiary anywhere in it.

The interface makes that arithmetic impossible to miss, because it is the whole argument. Type a ticker.

A name can carry separators, as on Runes. Press space and you get one. They are display only and cost nothing: DOG•GO•TO•THE•MOON and DOGGOTOTHEMOON are the same rune, priced on the same 14 characters, so a good ticker cannot be squatted a dozen times over by re-spacing it.

The design call: the release date is shown as a date, not as a duration. "Locked for 1460 days" is a number people skim past; "yours again on 8 August 2030" is a commitment they can picture, and it is the honest way to present a cost that is really a wait.

03

Etch a rune

One primitive covers both cases: supply 1 with divisibility 0 is a single item, supply N is a token. So there is one form, and fungibility is a choice inside it rather than two products.

The design call: every field that can never be changed is marked permanent, in the form, before the button. Not in a confirmation dialog people click through. An etching is immutable and a typo in the supply cap is forever.

04

Open mints and allowlists

An etching can carry mint terms and a 32-byte allowlist root. Thirty-two bytes buys a real airdrop or a fair whitelist, which neither BRC-20 nor Runes can express at all.

Each mint has a price, and the etcher sets it. It is paid as an ordinary transaction fee, so the miner of the block receives it and the protocol still names no beneficiary anywhere. Relay fees alone could not do this job: at 0.2 XVG/kB, taking a 21,000,000 supply costs about 2,100 XVG, which is a rounding error. At 20 XVG a mint, the same supply costs 420,000 XVG.

The protocol refuses to pick that number itself. Any constant it chose would be fixed in XVG and permanent from the first etching, while the thing it means is a value in dollars: 20 XVG is a few cents today, and a hundredfold move would price minting out of reach for reasons that have nothing to do with the rune. An etcher choosing on the day prices it at what XVG is worth on the day, and the interface suggests a figure so nobody has to guess.

This rations capital, not time, so it does not settle who gets there first. That is said here rather than fixed, because every rule that would settle a race rations time and so charges the ordinary minter for a problem they did not cause. The etcher picks: a list, a premine they distribute themselves, or an open race they went into with their eyes open.

The design call: the eligibility check runs before the button, not after the transaction. A wallet that lets you broadcast a mint you cannot make is a wallet that charges you a fee to be told no. It cannot always win: the last few mints of a capped supply are a race nobody can call in advance, so the screen has to be honest about that too rather than pretend the check is a guarantee. The price is on the button for the same reason. A fee is money that leaves and never comes back, so it belongs in the label, not in a confirmation dialog.

05

What holding one looks like

Balances live on outputs, like the coin itself, so an output can carry several runes at once and moving one is moving a UTXO. That is invisible most of the time and it matters in exactly one place: coin control.

The second card is the one this protocol makes necessary. A locked ticker price is still your money, and a wallet that does not show it has effectively taken it: you paid, the balance dropped, and nothing anywhere says it is coming back.

The design call: a coin carrying a rune is never auto-selected for change. The protocol is already safe by default (an ordinary send moves the whole balance to the recipient rather than destroying it), but "safe" here means your tokens land with whoever you paid. The wallet has to see them to keep them.

06

Selling one

Swaps are in the protocol rather than left to a marketplace: one partially signed transaction, the maker signs their leg, the taker appends theirs, and it either happens atomically or not at all. The same shape covers token for XVG, token for token, and item for token.

The design call: a listing sells a whole carrier, so the card leads with everything that carrier holds. Selling 500 GRUMPY and not noticing the Verginal riding on the same output is the mistake this screen exists to prevent.

07

What I deliberately left out

Unlisted and unannounced. Nothing here can move a coin: the page makes no wallet call and the protocol is switched off on this server. Sample figures throughout.
